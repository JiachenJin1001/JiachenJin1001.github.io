Design and methods

Earnings process
Minimum distance on fifteen variances and autocovariances by group; Arellano and Bond regression for persistence; fixed-effects regression for growth.
Premium
Correlated random effects with two measures of parental income; entropy balancing. 7 to 17 percent beyond parental income.
Return to college
Two-stage least squares with Zi = Intensityp(i) × Exposurec(i) and province and birth-year effects; inference for 29 provincial clusters.
Accounting
τ = βH ΔM + Direct and an Oaxaca and Blinder decomposition. The extra degrees of advantaged children account for 0.066 of the 0.072 log points.
Replication
Code and every file of estimates are public; scripts check each number printed in the paper.
Abstract

In the China Family Panel Studies, the children of officials, managers, and professionals (advantaged children) earn 22 percent more than other children (less advantaged children). I ask whether this advantage is built inside the labor market, through smaller earnings shocks or faster earnings growth, or before work begins, through access to college. The evidence points to the second. Covariance-structure and panel estimates show that the two groups' earnings processes do not differ: shocks have the same size and persistence, and earnings grow at the same rate. Holding parental income and demographics fixed by correlated random-effects regressions and entropy balancing leaves a premium of 7 to 17 percent. To learn whether the premium runs through college, I estimate the return to college from the 1999 university expansion, with an instrument that interacts provincial growth in seats with each cohort's exposure to the expansion. The added seats raised the chance of completing college by a similar amount in both groups, and for children who completed college because of them, college raised earnings by 0.68 log points, or 19 percent per year of college. A degree also closes the occupational gap between the groups; the point estimates suggest that college raises earnings more for advantaged children, while it raises the chance of holding an official, managerial, or professional job more for less advantaged children. Valued at the return to college, the gap in college completion accounts for three quarters to all of the premium, so what parental occupation passes on beyond income is mostly a four-year degree.
